Sudoku strategy: the four techniques that solve almost every puzzle

Sudoku never requires guessing if the puzzle is well-formed. Easy and medium grids from newspapers, apps, and our own Sudoku cartridge can be solved with just four ideas. Learn them in order, and you will stop getting stuck on the last three empty cells.

This guide is written for the version in the vault — 9x9, numbers 1-9, no timer — but the ideas work on any standard Sudoku.

1. Scanning — the 30-second sweep

Scanning is what beginners do naturally, but doing it systematically doubles its power. Pick a number, say 7. Look at every 3x3 box that already has a 7. That 7 eliminates its entire row and column in other boxes. In a box with only two empty cells left in the rows not blocked by a 7, you know 7 must go in one of them. Mark it mentally.

Do this for numbers 1-9, box by box. On an easy puzzle, scanning alone solves 40% of cells. The mistake is scanning randomly. Go in order: boxes 1 to 9, numbers 1 to 9. Your eyes learn the pattern.

Practice drill: Open a new Sudoku, set a 2-minute timer, and only place numbers you find by scanning. Don't use pencil marks yet. You will be surprised how far you get.

2. Pencil marks — write the maybes

When scanning stalls, write small candidates in each empty cell: which numbers could still go there? If a row already has 1,4,5, then an empty cell in that row cannot be 1,4,5. Do this for row, column, and box. Most players end up with 2-3 candidates per cell.

Yes, it looks messy. That mess is the puzzle telling you what it is. In our digital Sudoku you can toggle pencil mode — use it. On paper, write tiny numbers in the corner.

Once candidates are in, two patterns appear that scanning hides:

  • Naked single: A cell with only one candidate. That's the answer. Place it, then erase that candidate from its row, column, and box.
  • Hidden single: A candidate that appears only once in a row, column, or box, even though its cell has other candidates. Example: In row 3, only one cell can be 9, even though that cell also lists 2 and 6. That cell must be 9.

After every placement, update pencil marks. One placement often creates a chain of 3-4 more.

3. Naked pairs — the elimination trick

Look inside a single row, column, or box. If two cells have exactly the same two candidates — say both show {2,7} and nothing else — then 2 and 7 must occupy those two cells in some order. Therefore, you can erase 2 and 7 from all other cells in that same row, column, or box.

Example: In box 5, cells A and B both {3,8}. Even if you don't know which is which, no other cell in box 5 can be 3 or 8. Erase them. Often this reveals a naked single elsewhere.

Naked pairs work with triples too (three cells sharing three candidates), but pairs alone solve most medium puzzles. Search for them after pencil marks, not before.

4. Hidden singles — the quiet winner

Hidden singles are the most missed technique because the cell looks busy. Suppose in column 7, candidate 4 appears in pencil marks only in row 2, but row 2's cell lists {4,6,9}. You might ignore it because the cell has three options. Don't. If 4 has nowhere else to go in that column, that cell is 4.

How to spot them fast: For each number 1-9, count its appearances in each row, column, and box's pencil marks. If count = 1, you found a hidden single. In our Sudoku cartridge, you can highlight a number to see all its candidates — use that to count.

Hidden singles are why experts rarely guess. A puzzle that seems to need guessing usually hides two hidden singles.

Putting it together — a solving loop

Don't treat these as steps you do once. Loop:

  1. Scan for 2 minutes, place what you see.
  2. Add pencil marks to remaining empties.
  3. Find naked singles, place them, update marks.
  4. Find hidden singles, place them, update marks.
  5. Find naked pairs, erase candidates, go back to 3.

On easy puzzles, you will finish at step 3. On medium, you will loop 2-3 times. On hard, you may need X-Wing or Swordfish, but those are just bigger versions of naked pairs — same idea, more cells.

What about guessing? In a well-made Sudoku, if you reach a point where you think you must guess, you missed a hidden single. Go back and count candidates for each number again. It's almost always there.
