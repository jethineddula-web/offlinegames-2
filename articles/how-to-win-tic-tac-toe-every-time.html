How to never lose at Tic-Tac-Toe: the complete strategy

Tic-Tac-Toe is a solved game. If both players play perfectly, every game is a draw. That sounds boring, but it makes it the perfect game to teach a child strategy — because you can learn the perfect defense in ten minutes and then focus on exploiting mistakes.

This guide is for our Tic-Tac-Toe cartridge (neon grid, local hot-seat and vs bot), but it works on paper, chalk, or any version.

1. The opening: always take the center if you can

If you are X and go first, take the center. It gives you four winning lines and is part of every forced-draw strategy. If center is taken, take a corner — never an edge on first move. Edges look safe but create fewer threats and make forks harder to set up.

If you are O and opponent takes center, take a corner. If opponent takes a corner, take the center. If opponent takes an edge, take the center. The rule is simple: center > corner > edge.

Why corners beat edges: A corner participates in three lines (row, column, diagonal). An edge only participates in two. More lines means more chances to create two threats at once — called a fork.

2. The fork: the only way to force a win

A fork is a move that creates two winning threats simultaneously. Opponent can only block one, you win on the other.

Example: You have X in top-left and center. Opponent has O in top-middle. If you play bottom-right, you threaten both middle row (center + bottom-right needs bottom-middle) and diagonal (top-left + bottom-right needs center, but center is yours, so actually diagonal is blocked — bad example). Let's do a real one:

Board: X . O / . X . / . . O — If you play bottom-left, you threaten left column (X + bottom-left needs middle-left) and diagonal (X center + bottom-left needs top-right). O can only block one.

To set up forks, you need two non-blocked lines that intersect at your next move. Corners are best for this because they intersect row, column, and diagonal.

Practice: In our Tic-Tac-Toe, play vs bot on hard. The bot is programmed to block forks. Try to create a position where you have two lines with two X's and an empty third cell sharing a common empty cell.

3. Defense: block, then block the fork

Never lose means two rules:

  1. If opponent has two in a row with third empty, block it immediately.
  2. If opponent is setting up a fork, block the fork. How? Don't play an edge that lets opponent create two threats. The classic mistake: X center, O corner, X opposite corner, O plays edge — now X can fork. Correct defense is O should play edge that doesn't create fork opportunity.

The full perfect defense for O after X takes center: take a corner. If X then takes opposite corner, take any other corner — you force draw. If X takes edge, take corner that doesn't let X fork.

Memorizing this takes 5 minutes: Center, corner, block, block fork, center. That's the whole game.

4. How to beat a child (and teach them)

If you always draw, kids get bored. So teach them to spot the fork:

  • Let them go first and take center. You take corner.
  • When they don't block your fork, show them after: "See, I had two ways to win?"
  • Play best of 5, switch who starts. First to 3 wins, but draws don't count — this rewards attacking.

In classrooms, we use Tic-Tac-Toe to teach "if-then" thinking. Ask: "If I go here, what will they have to do? Then what will be open?" That's planning two moves ahead, which is the basis of chess and 2048.
